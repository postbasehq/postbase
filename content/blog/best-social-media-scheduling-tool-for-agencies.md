Postbase gives each client a workspace containing its channels, calendar, media and team. Admins manage the team; members can write and schedule. ## Which alternatives are better for approvals, reporting or branding?

### Sendible: when you want client management in the same tool

Sendible documents client dashboards for reviewing content, approval routing, permission groups, automated reports and a priority inbox. That makes it a useful first demo for an agency whose work continues after the post is scheduled.

Ask for a walkthrough using your actual client roles and reporting requirements. Its pricing page lists capabilities across tiers, so don't assume an entry subscription includes the whole workflow. [Sendible's plans and features](https://www.sendible.com/pricing).

### Planable: when sign-off is the bottleneck

Planable distinguishes optional, required and multi-level approvals. Basic offers optional approval; Pro adds required approval; Enterprise adds sequential approval stages. If an account lead and a client must approve in order, that distinction matters more than the word “collaboration.”

Its pricing is per workspace, with post and page limits. Analytics and inbox options also affect the bill. Price the required approval tier across every client workspace before comparing it with a flat subscription. [Planable's approval and plan details](https://planable.io/pricing/).

### SocialPilot: when you need account bundles and client review

SocialPilot Premium lists 25 social accounts, six users, bulk scheduling, client approval and white-label reports. Standard lists manager approval, which is a different requirement from external client sign-off.

Premium is listed at $100/month with monthly billing or $85/month billed annually. Extra accounts and users can change the total. It belongs on the shortlist when those bundled features match your delivery process. [SocialPilot pricing](https://www.socialpilot.co/plans).

### Cloud Campaign: when clients should see your agency's brand

Cloud Campaign lists unlimited users across its plans. Team includes standard white-labeling and five brand workspaces at $199/month with monthly billing; additional workspaces are listed at $29/month. Agency adds advanced white-labeling and paid social reporting.

Compare what “white-label” means in the demo: the login experience, reports and notifications may matter differently to your clients. A branded report alone may be enough; an agency-branded platform is a larger requirement. [Cloud Campaign's plans](https://www.cloudcampaign.com/plans).

### Sprout Social: when engagement and reporting justify a larger budget

Sprout Social Standard lists a consolidated inbox and collaboration tools with five profiles at $199 per seat/month, billed annually. Professional lists unlimited profiles and competitor, tag and paid insights at $299 per seat/month, billed annually.

That makes it worth evaluating when community management and reporting are billable services. Select the tier for the deliverables you sell, then multiply by the people who need access. Don't compare a publishing-only price with a plan chosen for engagement operations. [Sprout Social pricing](https://sproutsocial.com/pricing/).

### Buffer: when the client roster is small

Buffer Team includes unlimited team members, access levels and content approval workflows. Its displayed one-channel price is $10/month billed annually. Buffer also offers a free plan for three channels, with one user and ten scheduled posts per channel.

For a small roster, per-channel pricing can be practical. Use its calculator for your full account count; don't assume a one-channel quote is the price for an agency, or that one user is enough for client collaboration. [Buffer pricing](https://buffer.com/pricing).

## How should an agency compare the real monthly cost?

Write down five numbers before opening a pricing page: clients, connected accounts, internal users, client reviewers and monthly publishing volume. Then add the required approval steps, reporting outputs and any paid integrations. A cheap plan that misses one contractual requirement isn't a usable quote.

Consider an illustrative agency with **10 clients, three accounts per client and five internal staff**. It needs 30 connected accounts and, if it separates every client, 10 workspaces. Postbase Agency fits those capacity requirements; Pro's five-workspace limit does not, despite having enough channel capacity. That is a capacity calculation, not a claim that every part of the agency's workflow fits. [Plan limits](https://www.postbase.so/for/agencies).

Use the same brief with every vendor:

- **Per-channel pricing:** enter all 30 accounts and check volume discounts.
- **Per-workspace pricing:** price all 10 clients, including any reporting add-ons for each.
- **Per-seat pricing:** count everyone who needs paid access; check how external reviewers are treated.
- **Bundled pricing:** check every limit separately, including publishing volume and permissions.

Compare monthly billing with monthly billing. Show annual commitments separately. Include the staff time needed to collect approvals, assemble reports and handle failed posts; the subscription is only one part of the operating cost.

## What should you test before moving clients?

Run a small pilot with two client workspaces and a realistic batch of content. Use test accounts or explicitly approved posts for any publishing test.

1. **Separate clients.** Invite the intended roles and check exactly which calendars, assets and accounts each person can see.
2. **Complete a review cycle.** Draft, request changes, revise and approve. Check whether an edit after approval requires another sign-off, and who can bypass it.
3. **Test exact formats.** A supported network does not guarantee every format, account type, thumbnail, first comment or visibility setting you use.
4. **Handle a failure.** Ask the vendor to demonstrate an expired connection, notification and recovery flow. Identify who on your team owns the retry.
5. **Produce a client report.** Recreate one real deliverable and check the metrics, date range, export and branding.
6. **Rehearse offboarding.** Confirm how you remove a reviewer, disconnect accounts and retain the records your agency needs.

During migration, reconcile the old and new queues by client, account and timestamp. Assign one person to cancel duplicate scheduled posts and confirm the final calendar before switching publishing responsibility.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best social media scheduling tool for a small agency?

Choose around the work you deliver. Postbase is worth evaluating for separate client calendars with external sign-off. Buffer is a useful option for a small account roster; SocialPilot Premium is worth comparing when client approval and bulk scheduling are required. Test the full workflow before committing.

### Does Postbase have client approvals?

Not yet. Its documented process is to save drafts for review and schedule after approval. If the software must enforce sign-off, evaluate a tool with required approvals. [Postbase's agency FAQ](https://www.postbase.so/for/agencies).

### Does an AI scheduling integration replace client sign-off?

No. Generating copy and selecting a publishing time are separate from approving a client's message. Verify the account, final text, media, timezone and approval record before authorizing an assistant to schedule.

### Should an agency choose unlimited users or unlimited accounts?

Neither label decides the purchase. A team with many reviewers may value user capacity; an agency managing many brands may hit account or workspace limits first. Model your current roster and the next few clients, then check the remaining limits and required features.

### How do we decide between a scheduler and a full social suite?

Start with your client deliverables. If you mainly prepare and publish approved content, a scheduler may cover the job. If you also sell response management, listening or detailed reports, test those functions explicitly and budget for the appropriate tier.
